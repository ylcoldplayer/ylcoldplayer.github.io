A contraction you can hear

5 September, 2026 in reinforcement learning · Tags: dynamic programming, policy iteration

Dynamic programming has one theorem that does most of the work, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not give you. The short version: the Bellman operator is a contraction, so value iteration converges; but optimistic policy iteration is not the Bellman operator, and its convergence is a genuinely more delicate question.

The operator

Fix a discounted MDP with finite state space $\mathcal{S}$, finite action space $\mathcal{A}$, reward $r(s,a) \in [0, R]$, transition kernel $P(s' \mid s,a)$, and discount factor $\gamma \in (0,1)$. Define the Bellman optimality operator $T$ acting on $v \in \R^{\mathcal S}$ by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellman} (T v)(s) \;=\; \max_{a \in \mathcal A} \left\{ r(s,a) \;+\; \gamma \sum_{s' \in \mathcal S} P(s' \mid s, a)\, v(s') \right\} . \end{equation}

The basic fact is that $\eqref{eq:bellman}$ shrinks distances in the supremum norm.

Lemma 1. For all $v, w \in \R^{\mathcal S}$ we have $\norm{Tv - Tw}_\infty \le \gamma \norm{v - w}_\infty$.
Proof. Fix a state $s$ and let $a^\star$ attain the maximum in $(Tv)(s)$. Then $(Tw)(s) \ge r(s,a^\star) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a^\star) w(s')$, since the max is at least the value at $a^\star$. Subtracting, \[ (Tv)(s) - (Tw)(s) \;\le\; \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a^\star) \bigl( v(s') - w(s') \bigr) \;\le\; \gamma \norm{v-w}_\infty , \] using that $P(\cdot \mid s, a^\star)$ is a probability distribution. Exchanging the roles of $v$ and $w$ bounds the absolute difference, and taking the supremum over $s$ finishes the argument. $\square$

Banach's fixed point theorem now hands us everything at once: $T$ has a unique fixed point $v^\star$, the iterates $v_{k+1} = T v_k$ converge to it from any starting point, and the convergence is geometric at rate $\gamma$, with $\norm{v_k - v^\star}_\infty \le \gamma^k \norm{v_0 - v^\star}_\infty$. This is value iteration, and it is about as clean as an algorithm gets.

Where optimism enters

Policy iteration takes a different route. Given a policy $\pi$, let $T_\pi$ be the operator that evaluates it,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:eval} (T_\pi v)(s) \;=\; r(s, \pi(s)) \;+\; \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, \pi(s))\, v(s') , \end{equation}

which is also a $\gamma$-contraction, with fixed point $v^\pi$. Policy iteration alternates exact evaluation ($v \leftarrow v^\pi$, i.e. applying $T_\pi$ to convergence) with greedy improvement ($\pi \leftarrow \argmax$ w.r.t. $v$). Optimistic policy iteration (OPI) does the impatient thing and truncates the evaluation after finitely many sweeps:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:opi} \pi_k \in \mathcal{G}(v_k), \qquad v_{k+1} = T_{\pi_k}^{\,m_k} v_k , \end{equation}

where $\mathcal{G}(v)$ denotes the set of policies greedy with respect to $v$ and $m_k \ge 1$ is the number of evaluation sweeps. Setting $m_k = 1$ recovers value iteration; letting $m_k \to \infty$ recovers policy iteration. The whole family sits in between, and in practice one always runs something in between.

Remark 2. It is tempting to conclude from Lemma 1 that $\eqref{eq:opi}$ inherits the contraction and therefore converges. It does not follow. The map $v \mapsto T_{\mathcal{G}(v)}^{\,m} v$ is a composition of a contraction with a discontinuous selection: the greedy set $\mathcal{G}(v)$ jumps as $v$ crosses a tie between actions. Each individual operator is well behaved; the composition need not be, and the map is not in general a contraction in any norm.

With synchronous updates and a uniform sweep count this is still fine — monotonicity arguments rescue the conclusion, provided $v_0$ is initialised below $T v_0$. The situation gets genuinely harder once the updates become asynchronous or stochastic, so that different states are evaluated a different number of times and the sweep counts $m_k$ vary across the state space. Then the monotonicity argument loses its footing, and whether the iterates converge becomes sensitive to how the step sizes are chosen.

That sensitivity is not merely a gap in the analysis. I constructed an explicit counterexample for the scalar-stepsize nonuniform Monte Carlo variant in arXiv:2606.15978, and the earlier convergence question for the stochastic shortest path setting is treated in arXiv:1808.08763. The moral I take from both: the contraction property is a statement about a single operator, and almost every practical algorithm is a composition of several. The composition is where the difficulty lives.
