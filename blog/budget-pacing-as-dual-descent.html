Budget pacing as dual gradient descent

18 September, 2026 in advertising, optimization · Tags: budget pacing, duality, online learning

A recurring question from engineers new to ads systems is why budget pacing controllers all end up looking the same: keep a single scalar, shade every bid by it, and nudge it up or down according to whether you are overspending. The answer is that this is exactly online gradient descent on the dual of a linear program, and the scalar is the Lagrange multiplier on the budget constraint. I want to write that derivation down carefully, because once you see it the design choices in a real pacing system stop looking arbitrary.

The fluid relaxation

Fix a single advertiser facing $T$ auction opportunities. At step $t$ the advertiser observes a value $v_t \ge 0$ for the impression, and the highest competing bid is $p_t \ge 0$. We assume a truthful (second-price) auction, so submitting a bid $b_t$ wins the impression precisely when $b_t > p_t$, and the advertiser then pays $p_t$. The advertiser has a budget $B$ for the horizon.

Write $x_t \in \set{0,1}$ for the indicator that we win opportunity $t$. If we knew the whole sequence $(v_t, p_t)_{t=1}^T$ in advance and were allowed to fractionally allocate, the best achievable utility would be the value of the linear program

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fluid} \begin{aligned} \max_{x \in [0,1]^T} \quad & \sum_{t=1}^T (v_t - p_t)\, x_t \\ \text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{t=1}^T p_t\, x_t \le B . \end{aligned} \end{equation}

This is the fluid or hindsight benchmark. It is a relaxation in two senses: it knows the future, and it allows fractional wins. Both make it optimistic, which is what we want from a benchmark.

Dualising the budget

Attach a multiplier $\lambda \ge 0$ to the budget constraint in $\eqref{eq:fluid}$. The Lagrangian is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrangian} L(x, \lambda) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T (v_t - p_t)\, x_t \;-\; \lambda\left( \sum_{t=1}^T p_t\, x_t - B \right) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \bigl( v_t - (1+\lambda)\, p_t \bigr) x_t \;+\; \lambda B . \end{equation}

The point of $\eqref{eq:lagrangian}$ is that the budget coupling is gone: the sum decouples across $t$. For a fixed $\lambda$, maximising over $x \in [0,1]^T$ is done independently at each step, and the maximiser is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule} x_t = 1 \iff v_t - (1+\lambda) p_t > 0 \iff p_t < \frac{v_t}{1+\lambda} . \end{equation}

Now compare $\eqref{eq:rule}$ with the auction rule. We win exactly when $b_t > p_t$. So the allocation prescribed by the dual is implemented exactly by submitting the shaded bid

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bid} b_t \;=\; \frac{v_t}{1 + \lambda} . \end{equation}
Remark 1. This is the whole reason pacing is implemented as a multiplicative shade rather than, say, a probabilistic throttle. Shading by $1/(1+\lambda)$ is not a heuristic — it is the unique bid that makes a truthful auction reproduce the dual-optimal allocation $\eqref{eq:rule}$. Throttling (dropping a random fraction of auctions) implements a different, generally worse, allocation, because it discards high-value and low-value opportunities at the same rate.

Learning the multiplier online

In practice we do not know the right $\lambda$, because it depends on the competitive landscape over the whole horizon. But the dual function

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual} D(\lambda) \;=\; \max_{x \in [0,1]^T} L(x, \lambda) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \bigl( v_t - (1+\lambda) p_t \bigr)^+ \;+\; \lambda B \end{equation}

is convex in $\lambda$ (a supremum of affine functions), so we can run gradient descent on it. Differentiating $\eqref{eq:dual}$, a subgradient is $B - \sum_t p_t x_t$: the budget minus the spend. Splitting this across the $T$ steps and writing $\rho = B/T$ for the target spend per opportunity, the per-step stochastic subgradient is $\rho - p_t x_t$. Descending it, and projecting onto $\lambda \ge 0$, gives the update

\begin{equation}\label{eq:update} \lambda_{t+1} \;=\; \Bigl[\, \lambda_t \;-\; \eta \bigl( \rho - z_t \bigr) \Bigr]^+ , \qquad z_t = p_t x_t , \end{equation}

where $z_t$ is the realised spend at step $t$ and $\eta > 0$ is a step size. Read $\eqref{eq:update}$ in plain language: if we spent more than the target rate ($z_t > \rho$), then $\lambda$ increases, bids get shaded harder, and we slow down. If we underspent, $\lambda$ decreases and we bid closer to value. That is a pacing controller, and we did not have to invent it.

The resulting algorithm is a handful of lines:

lam = 0.0
rho = B / T                      # target spend per opportunity
for t in range(T):
    v = value_model(request_t)
    bid = v / (1.0 + lam)        # eq. (4)
    won, paid = auction(bid)
    z = paid if won else 0.0
    lam = max(0.0, lam - eta * (rho - z))   # eq. (6)

Why this is a good controller

The reason to prefer this derivation over a hand-tuned PID loop is that it comes with a guarantee. Suppose the pairs $(v_t, p_t)$ are drawn i.i.d. from some unknown distribution, and let $\mathrm{OPT}$ denote the expected value of the fluid benchmark $\eqref{eq:fluid}$.

Theorem 2 (Balseiro–Gur). Run the bidding rule $\eqref{eq:bid}$ with the dual update $\eqref{eq:update}$ and step size $\eta = \Theta(1/\sqrt{T})$. Then the expected utility collected over the horizon is at least $\mathrm{OPT} - O(\sqrt{T})$, and the budget is never exceeded. The $\sqrt{T}$ rate is optimal: no algorithm, even one that knows the distribution, can do better than $\Omega(\sqrt{T})$ in the worst case.

I will not reproduce the full argument, but the shape of it is worth internalising, because it explains the step size.

Proof sketch. Weak duality gives $\mathrm{OPT} \le D(\lambda)$ for every $\lambda \ge 0$, so it suffices to control how far the iterates $\lambda_t$ are from the minimiser of $D$. The update $\eqref{eq:update}$ is projected online gradient descent on a convex function whose subgradients are bounded by $\max(\rho, \bar p)$, where $\bar p$ is a bound on prices. Standard OGD analysis then yields a regret of $O(\eta T + 1/\eta)$ against the best fixed multiplier, and balancing the two terms gives $\eta \asymp 1/\sqrt{T}$ with value $O(\sqrt{T})$. The budget is respected by construction: once spend reaches $B$ the advertiser exits, and the analysis accounts for the truncated horizon separately. $\square$

Two practical consequences fall out of the balancing step. First, the right step size scales with the inverse square root of the number of opportunities, not with wall-clock time — a campaign seeing $10^6$ requests per hour and one seeing $10^3$ need very different $\eta$. Second, because the guarantee is against a fixed multiplier, the controller is only as good as the stationarity of the environment; when the landscape shifts intraday you want a constant step size rather than a decaying one, trading asymptotic optimality for tracking ability.

The multiplier is not a tuning knob. It is a price — specifically, the shadow price of the budget constraint — and the controller is a mechanism for discovering it.

Where the model breaks

The derivation above leans on assumptions that are worth naming, since each one is a place where production systems diverge:

  • Truthfulness. Equation $\eqref{eq:bid}$ relies on the auction being second-price. Under first-price rules the mapping from bid to allocation changes, and the optimal shade depends on a bid landscape model rather than on $\lambda$ alone.
  • A single constraint. With a budget, a ROI target, and frequency caps, $\lambda$ becomes a vector and $\eqref{eq:update}$ becomes a projection onto a more interesting set.
  • i.i.d. arrivals. Real traffic is strongly diurnal. Theorem 2 does not apply as stated, which is why production pacers layer a forecast of the daily traffic curve on top of $\rho$.
  • Value known at bid time. In practice $v_t$ is itself a model output with its own error, and that error interacts with the shading in ways the LP does not see.
